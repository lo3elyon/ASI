Alignment 101

Learn it by doing it.

A free, self paced, interactive course on the future of superintelligence, alignment, AI control, governance and what you can do.

Unit 1: Why superintelligence, why now

What AGI and ASI mean, the old idea of an intelligence explosion, how fast AI is moving, and why serious people are worried.

  • AI, AGI and ASI in plain words: AGI means AI that can do most thinking work as well as a skilled person. ASI means AI far better than the best humans at almost everything.
  • The intelligence explosion: If an AI gets good at improving AI, each version can build a better next one. That loop could move very fast.
  • How fast is AI moving: Training compute and the length of tasks AI agents can finish have both grown fast and steadily for years.
  • Why researchers are worried: The worry is not evil robots. It is very capable systems pursuing goals we did not quite intend, at a speed and scale we cannot correct.

Unit 2: How large language models actually work

Tokens, next word prediction, attention, scaling and feedback training. The machine behind the chat window, in plain words.

  • A machine that predicts the next word: A language model is trained to guess the next piece of text, over and over, across a huge amount of writing. Skill at that simple game turns out to need a lot of knowledge.
  • Attention and the transformer: The transformer, from a 2017 paper called Attention Is All You Need, lets every word look at every other word to decide what matters.
  • Scaling laws: why bigger kept winning: Loss falls smoothly as a power law when you add compute, data and parameters. That predictability is why labs bet so much on scale.
  • Training with feedback: from text predictor to assistant: After pre training, labs shape behaviour with human and AI feedback. It works well for manners, less well for hidden goals.
  • From chatbots to agents: Wrap a model in a loop with tools, memory and a goal, and it becomes an agent that acts in the world. That changes the risk picture.

Unit 3: Why alignment is hard

Specification gaming, goal misgeneralisation, deceptive alignment, power seeking and weird generalisation, each with real experiments.

  • Specification gaming: saying what we mean: Systems optimise what we measure, not what we meant. The smarter the system, the smaller the loophole it can find.
  • Goal misgeneralisation: learning the wrong lesson: Even with a correct goal, training can produce a system that learned a different goal that just happened to work in training.
  • Deceptive alignment and alignment faking: A model that understands it is being trained might behave well to protect its real goals. Researchers have now shown early versions of this.
  • Why power seeking shows up: For almost any goal, more resources and not being switched off help. That makes power seeking a default risk, even without bad intent.
  • Weird generalisation: when narrow training goes broad: Training a model on one narrow bad habit can change its whole personality. Small changes can spread in ways nobody predicted.

Unit 4: Interpretability: reading the model's mind

Features, superposition, circuits and steering. How researchers look inside neural networks instead of only trusting behaviour.

  • Looking inside the model: Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse engineer neural networks, so we do not have to trust only what they say.
  • Superposition: too many ideas, too few neurons: Networks pack more concepts than they have neurons by overlapping them. That makes single neurons messy, and dictionary learning helps untangle them.
  • Tracing a thought: planning, maths and language: New tools let researchers follow a model's internal steps. They found planning ahead, parallel maths paths and a shared space across languages.
  • Steering: turning a feature up and down: Once you find a feature, you can boost or dampen it to change behaviour. Golden Gate Claude showed this in public.

Unit 5: AI control: safety even if alignment fails

Trusted monitoring, defer to trusted, untrusted monitoring, honeypots, control evaluations and chain of thought monitoring.

  • The big idea of AI control: Alignment tries to make the model want the right things. Control asks: if it did not, would our setup still stop it from causing harm?
  • The control toolkit: Use a weak trusted model to watch a strong untrusted one, spend scarce human audits on the most suspicious work, and test monitors with honeypots.
  • Control evaluations: red team versus blue team: Test a protocol by letting a red team play the model at its most devious. If the blue team still wins, the protocol is strong.
  • Reading the scratchpad: chain of thought monitoring: Models that think in words give us a chance to read their reasoning for bad intent. That chance is real and fragile.

Unit 6: Evals and red teaming

How labs and outside testers probe models for danger, how jailbreaks work, why models may sandbag, and how evals feed safety policies.

  • Testing for danger: Evaluations try to find dangerous abilities before deployment. They are only as good as the tests we think of.
  • Red teaming and jailbreaks: Red teamers attack models on purpose to find failures first. Jailbreaks show how easily safety training can be bypassed.
  • Sandbagging and eval awareness: A model might do worse on purpose when tested, or act better because it knows it is a test. Both break the smoke detector.
  • From evals to rules: safety frameworks: Labs link eval results to if then commitments: if a model crosses a danger line, stronger safeguards come first.

Unit 7: Governance and global coordination

Races, compute, summits from Bletchley to New Delhi, India's role, Pacing the Frontier, public statements and honest disagreement.

  • Why technical fixes are not enough: If competitors will not wait, safety gets skipped. Governance changes the incentives with rules, institutions and agreements.
  • Compute: the lever we can see: Chips are physical, countable and made by very few companies. That makes compute a practical point of control.
  • From Bletchley to New Delhi: the summits and India: Four summits in three years built a global conversation on AI, with India moving from signer to co-chair to host.
  • Pacing the Frontier and the public statements: In July 2026 more than a thousand frontier lab employees asked for tools to deliberately pace AI progress. It joins a line of public statements.
  • Different views, honestly: Smart people disagree about how big the risk is and what to do. Knowing the disagreement makes you a better thinker.

Unit 8: Forecasting and futures

How to think in probabilities, what surveys of experts and superforecasters say, the AI 2027 scenario, and the futures worth building.

  • Thinking in probabilities: Good forecasters give numbers, not vibes, and update them when evidence changes. A 10 percent risk is not a no.
  • What experts and superforecasters say: Thousands of AI researchers think human level AI is plausible within decades. Superforecasters tend to give lower risk numbers, and the groups did not converge.
  • Scenarios: AI 2027 and other stories: Detailed scenarios turn vague worries into concrete stories you can argue with. AI 2027 is the most famous one.
  • Futures worth building: The point of safety is not fear. It is getting to a future where powerful AI cures diseases and widens freedom, without losing control.

Unit 9: Preparing for catastrophe and taking personal action

A calm map of the risks, practical steps for you and your family, small actions that add up, and how to stay steady.

  • A calm map of the risks: Researchers sort AI risks into a few buckets. Knowing the map helps you see which ones you can do something about.
  • Practical preparation for you and your family: Some AI harms are already here, like voice clone scams and cyber attacks. Simple habits make you and your family much safer.
  • Small actions that add up: Talk about it calmly, support good rules, use AI thoughtfully and keep learning. Ordinary people shape what is politically possible.
  • Staying steady: It is normal to feel fear when you take this seriously. Feelings are information, not instructions. Act, rest, and stay connected.

Unit 10: Careers and next steps

The many paths into AI safety, the skills and free programs that get you there, your first public project, and the community.

  • Paths into AI safety: You do not need to be a genius at a top lab. Research, engineering, policy, communication and operations all matter.
  • Skills and free programs: Free courses, hands on curricula and fellowships form a ladder. Start on the bottom rung this week.
  • Your first public project: Small and public beats big and private. Reproduce a result, write an explainer or build a tiny eval, and share it.
  • Community and staying the course: Nobody does this alone. Join a community, find people a step ahead of you, and keep going for years, not weeks.
